Kueh Quest!

A game about CPF's digital services

Duration

Feb 2022 – Jul 2022 (6 months)

Team

5 students

My role

UX research & design, visual design, front-end development, usability testing

Tools

Miro, Figma, Canva, ReactJS, Tailwind CSS, AntDesign, Git

Project summary

As part of my undergraduate Capstone project, I teamed up with a group of 5 in collaboration with the Central Provident Fund (CPF) Board to come up with a solution to increase take-up of CPF's digital services.

I contributed to UX research and design during the initial stage of the project, and later to the front-end development of the web application.

The problem

Currently, the CPF Board (CPFB) sees its members aged 60 and above going to the 5 Service Centres around Singapore to access CPF e-Services, such as accessing their Statement of Account and making a CPF top-up.

CPFB wants to encourage more members to access these e-Services on their own, online. However, there is a need to:

  • Improve members' confidence with self-help
  • Improve accessibility and convenience for members
Members aged 60 and above visit CPF's Service Centres for help with simple transactions that can actually be done online on their own.

However, the majority of members aged 60 and above are less tech-savvy, with a lack of confidence and knowledge in accessing the CPF website and its services.

Our solution

Kueh Quest! is a web-based game that simulates the CPF website and incorporates gamification elements to engage CPF members aged 60 and above. The game can be accessed via a mobile phone or computer.

The aim of the game is to equip CPF members with the necessary know-how to navigate and perform common CPF e-Services.

The process

1. Empathise

Focus group discussions

Two online focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with CPF Customer Service Executives (CSEs), aiming to:

  • Understand CSEs' daily work processes/procedures
  • Learn about the types of enquiries encountered by members and how they're handled
  • Identify reasons why the target group of CPF members prefer to visit the service centre / call, instead of choosing self-help options like the CPF website
Online Focus Group Discussions were held with CPF staff
My team visited CPF's service centers to conduct interviews with CPF members aged 50 and above.

One-to-one interviews

After the FGDs, my team conducted interviews with 30 CPF members aged above 50 at CPF service centres around Singapore, to build on the insights gathered regarding possible barriers preventing elderly members from using the CPF website.

Questions covered included:

  • Demographics (e.g. age range, access to digital devices)
  • Past experience with CPF's digital services
  • Scenario tasks navigating a specific service on the website

I assisted with conducting the interviews and noting down key verbatims.

Key verbatims from our one-to-one interview sessions at CPF Service Centres

2. Define

Affinity mapping

I guided my team in conducting affinity mapping for the verbatims gathered from both the FGDs and one-to-one interviews, grouping similar verbatims together to uncover key insights.

The team identified three main barriers preventing elderly members from adopting the CPF website:

Lack of confidence

Older members lacked confidence accessing the CPF website and its digital services.

Lack of help

Little guidance was provided to older members on how to use the CPF website.

Fear of scams

No concrete way to address elderly members' fears towards online scams.

We refined our problem statement to guide our solution ideation:

"We aim to address the barriers faced by less tech-savvy elderly CPF members in their transition to adopting technologies, by designing a solution that systematically guides these members to pick up basic digital navigation skills, to prepare them for navigating and adopting the CPF website."

3. Ideation

Concept generation through research

My team researched existing solutions tackling similar barriers to digital adoption among the elderly. Based on our literature findings, we identified five solution approaches: online guides, training programs, gamification, peer-to-peer learning, and elderly-friendly user interfaces.

  1. Online Guides
  2. Training Programs
  3. Gamification
  4. Peer-to-peer Learning
  5. Elderly-friendly User Interface
Mindmap done by the team to generate possible solutions

Brainstorming & evaluation

My team then used a mindmap to brainstorm design ideas, branching out from each of the 5 solution approaches.

Solution Evaluation

With the potential solutions conceived, we evaluated their efficacy and feasibility using a Pugh chart.

Pugh chart created to evaluate our solution ideas

Selected Solution

From the Pugh Chart, the solution of a website training simulator received the highest score, so we proceeded with it.

Originating from the "gamification / serious game" approach, we devised a simulation of the CPF website, including gamification elements such as a storyline and reward mechanisms.

4. Prototyping

Low-fidelity wireframes

Using Figma, I assisted my team in drafting low-fidelity wireframes of how the website training simulator would look — such as the main page and the guide feature. From our literature review, we ensured to incorporate elderly-friendly UI elements such as a simplified layout and large buttons.

Design Iteration 1
Design Iteration 2 - Mobile layout

High-fidelity wireframes

From our interviews with the CPF members, the team found that 19 out of 20 had smartphones, while only 10 had computers.

Noting that the majority use smartphones rather than desktops, for our next iteration we changed our UI design to a mobile version to better suit our target users.

Improving the game narrative

While our in-game story worked reasonably well in illustrating the benefits of using the CPF website, project mentors gave feedback that the story lacked engagement.

To improve attractiveness, we used nostalgic elements — a proven method for improving game engagement for older users (Hartmann & Brunk, 2019)

We ultimately settled on the theme of food — specifically kueh, a bite-sized local snack that holds a dear place in many Singaporeans' hearts — believing it would add an element of nostalgia to the story and make it more appealing to elderly members.

Our team incorporated "kueh" as a theme, building on nostalgia to increase the game's appeal toward our target users
Iteration 3 - Minor UI improvements

Preliminary testing & iteration

After brief user testing with 10 CPF members, feedback showed some members wanted to backtrack to the previous page to better understand the series of clicks required to complete a quest.

For the next iteration, we added a "back" button during quest playthrough (for the usability heuristic of flexibility) and a "home" button to return to the quest selection page.

Development

Once the UI designs were finalised, my team moved on to coding out the web app. I contributed mainly to front-end development and deployment:

  • ReactJS — coded out various components of the game's front end
  • Tailwind CSS — used to ease UI development
  • AntDesign — UI components for a consistent visual design
  • Heroku + Netlify — deployed the frontend on Netlify and the backend on Heroku

5. Testing

Quantitative testing

To ascertain how well members were playing our game, the team based this on the number of wrong clicks for each game quest, captured automatically through the game platform.

A total of 20 CPF members took part. Based on the results, the team concluded that the in-game guides were adequately successful in minimising wrong clicks — providing a simple and effective method for teaching users to navigate CPF e-Services.

Results for the number of incorrect clicks for each quest

Qualitative study

After quantitative testing, participants shared their experience and any areas for improvement. Most expressed satisfaction with the game — not just the step-by-step guide, but also the engaging story and the in-game "kueh" rewards.

Some verbatims from our post-testing interview

Future work

While heartened by the positive results, the team noted plenty of room for improvement, including:

Different language options

To allow users to play the game in their preferred mother tongue.

Audio-based features

Narrations for the story and quest playthrough, for users with limited vision and auditory learners.

More quest variety

A wider range of other common CPF e-Services represented as quests.

Conclusion

From this project, I learnt the importance of putting myself in the users' shoes and thoroughly understanding their needs. Having to dig deep to uncover our users' existing preconceptions and behaviours was a lengthy and challenging process, but a fulfilling one.

I also picked up new skills such as front-end development with ReactJS and its libraries, as well as deployment with Heroku and Netlify. Though the timeline was tight with plenty of ups and downs, I'm proud of my team for pulling through and landing on a well-rounded solution.
